GI Health/Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Inflammatory Bowel Disease

An umbrella term for long-term conditions that cause inflammation of the digestive tract — most commonly Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.

What it is

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) describes a group of conditions in which the immune system causes ongoing inflammation of the gut. Unlike a short-lived tummy upset, this inflammation is chronic — it tends to come and go in flares followed by periods of remission.

IBD is not the same as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). IBD involves measurable inflammation and tissue damage, whereas IBS is a disorder of how the gut functions without that visible damage.

Good to know: there is currently no cure for IBD, but modern treatment can control inflammation, ease symptoms and keep many people in long, stable remission.

Types of IBD

Crohn's disease

Can affect any part of the gut from mouth to anus, most often the end of the small intestine.

Ulcerative colitis

Limited to the large intestine (colon) and rectum, causing inflammation and ulcers in the lining.

Microscopic colitis

Inflammation of the colon seen only under a microscope, causing watery, non-bloody diarrhoea.

Crohn's and ulcerative colitis are the two main forms. In a small number of people the pattern doesn't fit neatly into either and is described as IBD-unclassified.

Symptoms

Symptoms vary with the type of IBD and which part of the gut is affected, but commonly include:

  • Persistent diarrhoea, sometimes with blood or mucus
  • Tummy (abdominal) pain and cramping
  • Tiredness and fatigue
  • Unintended weight loss and loss of appetite
  • A frequent or urgent need to empty the bowel

IBD can also cause symptoms outside the gut, such as joint pain, mouth ulcers, and red or sore eyes.

Causes & risk factors

The exact cause is not fully understood. IBD is thought to develop when the immune system over-reacts in people who are genetically susceptible, triggered by factors in the environment and the gut's own bacteria (the microbiome).

  • Family history — IBD is more common if a close relative has it
  • Age — most often diagnosed between the late teens and 30s
  • Smoking — increases the risk and severity of Crohn's disease
  • Environment — IBD is more common in industrialised countries

Diagnosis

No single test confirms IBD. A diagnosis is usually built up from several:

  • Blood tests to check for inflammation and anaemia
  • A stool test for faecal calprotectin, a marker of gut inflammation
  • Endoscopy (colonoscopy) to look directly at the bowel lining and take biopsies
  • Scans such as MRI or CT to assess the small bowel

Treatment & management

Treatment has two goals: settling a flare, then maintaining remission. The right approach depends on the type, location and severity of the disease.

  • Anti-inflammatory medicines to calm the gut lining
  • Immune-modifying and biologic therapies for moderate-to-severe disease
  • Short courses of steroids to bring a flare under control
  • Surgery for some people when medicines aren't enough
  • Diet, stop-smoking support and wellbeing care alongside medical treatment

Most people with IBD are cared for by a specialist team and have regular reviews to keep the condition under control.
