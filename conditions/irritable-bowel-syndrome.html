GI Health/Irritable Bowel Syndrome

Irritable Bowel Syndrome

A common, long-term disorder of how the gut functions — causing abdominal pain, bloating and changes in bowel habit, without visible damage to the bowel.

What it is

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is one of the most commonly diagnosed digestive conditions. It is a functional disorder, which means the gut doesn't work as it should even though it looks normal and is not damaged. This sets it apart from inflammatory bowel disease.

IBS is a real, physical condition — not something that is "all in the mind". It is closely linked to the communication between the gut and the brain, sometimes called the gut–brain axis. While it can be uncomfortable and disruptive, it does not damage the bowel or raise the risk of bowel cancer.

Subtypes

IBS is grouped by the main change in bowel habit, which helps guide treatment:

IBS-D

Diarrhoea-predominant — loose or frequent stools are the main problem.

IBS-C

Constipation-predominant — infrequent, hard or difficult-to-pass stools.

IBS-M

Mixed — alternating between diarrhoea and constipation over time.

Symptoms

  • Abdominal pain or cramping, often relieved by passing a stool
  • Bloating and a swollen tummy
  • Diarrhoea, constipation, or both at different times
  • Excess wind
  • A feeling of not having fully emptied the bowel
  • Mucus in the stool
Always get checked: blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, a persistent change in bowel habit over the age of 50, or symptoms that wake you at night are not typical of IBS and should be assessed by a doctor.

Causes & triggers

There is no single cause. IBS is thought to involve an oversensitive gut, changes in how quickly the bowel moves, and altered gut–brain signalling. Common triggers that worsen symptoms include:

  • Diet — certain foods, including some high in fermentable carbohydrates (FODMAPs)
  • Stress and anxiety, through the gut–brain connection
  • A previous bout of gastroenteritis (post-infectious IBS)
  • Hormonal changes, such as around the menstrual cycle

Diagnosis

IBS is usually diagnosed from the typical pattern of symptoms (often using the Rome criteria) once other conditions have been ruled out. A doctor may arrange blood tests, a coeliac disease test and a stool test for faecal calprotectin to help exclude inflammation or infection.

Treatment & management

IBS cannot be cured, but symptoms can usually be well controlled with a combination of approaches tailored to the individual:

  • Diet and lifestyle — regular meals, adjusting fibre, limiting caffeine and alcohol; a dietitian-supervised low-FODMAP diet helps many people
  • Medicines — antispasmodics for pain, laxatives for constipation or anti-diarrhoeals for loose stools
  • Probiotics — worth a trial for some
  • Psychological therapies — cognitive behavioural therapy or gut-directed hypnotherapy can be very effective

Living with it

Identifying your personal triggers — often with a food and symptom diary — managing stress, staying active and keeping a regular routine can make a big difference. IBS often follows a relapsing pattern, but most people find a management plan that lets them get on with daily life.
