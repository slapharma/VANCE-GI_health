Ulcerative Colitis

A type of inflammatory bowel disease that causes inflammation and ulcers in the lining of the colon and rectum.

What it is

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a long-term condition where the lining of the large bowel, the colon and rectum, becomes inflamed. Small sores (ulcers) can form on the lining, which may bleed and produce mucus.

UC always starts in the rectum and can spread part or all of the way up the colon. Unlike Crohn's disease, it affects only the colon and rectum, and only the inner lining.

Most people have times when symptoms flare up, followed by periods of remission when they feel well.

Symptoms

  • Diarrhoea, often containing blood or mucus
  • An urgent and frequent need to empty the bowel
  • Tummy pain and cramping, often eased by passing a stool
  • A feeling of needing to go even when the bowel is empty (tenesmus)
  • Tiredness, weight loss and reduced appetite during flares
When to seek help: it's a good idea to seek urgent advice if you have a severe flare, for example frequent bloody stools, a fever, a racing heart or significant tummy pain.

Causes & risk factors

UC develops from a combination of factors, rather than one single cause:

  • Genetics and family history of IBD
  • The immune system's response in the gut
  • The gut microbiome
  • Environmental factors
A note on smoking: smoking affects the two main types of IBD differently. It increases the risk and severity of Crohn's disease, but ulcerative colitis is more common in people who have never smoked or who have stopped. This is not a reason to start or keep smoking, which harms health in many other ways.

Diagnosis

  • Blood tests for inflammation and anaemia
  • Stool tests, including faecal calprotectin, and tests to rule out infection
  • Colonoscopy with biopsies to confirm the diagnosis and map how much of the colon is involved

Treatment & management

The aim is to bring flares under control, then keep the bowel healed and in remission.

  • Aminosalicylates (5-ASAs): often the first-line treatment, taken by mouth or as an enema/suppository
  • Steroids: short courses to settle a flare
  • Immunosuppressants and biologic therapies: for more active or hard-to-treat disease
  • Surgery: removing the colon can cure UC and is an option for some people

Living with it

Many people with UC lead full, active lives. Taking maintenance treatment as prescribed, attending reviews, knowing your personal warning signs and looking after sleep, stress and diet all help keep things stable.
