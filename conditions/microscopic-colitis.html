Microscopic Colitis

Inflammation of the colon that can only be seen under a microscope, causing chronic, watery, non-bloody diarrhoea.

What it is

Microscopic colitis is a type of inflammatory bowel disease affecting the large bowel. Its defining feature is that the colon usually looks completely normal during a colonoscopy. The inflammation only shows up when a small tissue sample (a biopsy) is examined under a microscope.

Because the bowel looks normal, it is often mistaken for irritable bowel syndrome. It is a common and treatable cause of ongoing watery diarrhoea, particularly in older adults.

Subtypes

SubtypeWhat's different under the microscope
Collagenous colitisA thickened band of collagen forms just beneath the lining of the colon
Lymphocytic colitisAn increased number of white blood cells (lymphocytes) in the colon lining

The two subtypes cause similar symptoms and are generally treated in the same way.

Symptoms

  • Chronic watery diarrhoea that is not bloody
  • An urgent need to empty the bowel, sometimes at night
  • Tummy pain or cramps
  • Faecal incontinence in some people
  • Tiredness and mild weight loss

Causes & risk factors

The exact trigger isn't fully understood, but it is thought to involve the immune system reacting to something passing through the bowel. Microscopic colitis is more likely in people with these known associations:

  • Being female and over 60
  • Autoimmune conditions such as coeliac disease, thyroid disorders and rheumatoid arthritis
  • Certain medicines, including some anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs), acid-reducing drugs (PPIs) and some antidepressants (SSRIs)
  • Smoking

Diagnosis

Diagnosis relies on a colonoscopy with biopsies. Even when the bowel looks normal, the laboratory findings confirm the type. Blood and stool tests help rule out other causes such as coeliac disease or infection.

Why it can be missed: a normal-looking colonoscopy can be falsely reassuring. In anyone with persistent watery diarrhoea, taking biopsies is the key step that confirms the diagnosis.

Treatment & management

  • Budesonide, a targeted steroid, is usually the first-line treatment and works well for most people
  • Reviewing any medicines that may be contributing
  • Anti-diarrhoeal medicines for milder symptoms
  • Stopping smoking and simple dietary adjustments

Symptoms often settle with treatment, though they can come back and may need a further course.
