C. difficile Infection

A bacterial infection of the bowel — often triggered when antibiotics disturb the balance of the gut's natural bacteria.

Conditions

What it is

Clostridioides difficile (often shortened to C. diff) is a type of bacteria that can live harmlessly in the gut alongside many others. Problems arise when the natural balance of gut bacteria — the microbiome — is disturbed, allowing C. diff to multiply and release toxins that inflame the bowel.

The result is an infection that ranges from mild diarrhoea to a serious inflammation of the colon. It most often affects older adults in hospitals or care homes, particularly after a course of antibiotics.

Symptoms

  • Watery diarrhoea, often several times a day
  • Tummy pain and cramping
  • A high temperature (fever)
  • Loss of appetite and feeling sick

Symptoms can begin within a day or two of taking antibiotics, but may not appear until weeks or even a couple of months afterwards.

Causes & risk factors

  • Recent antibiotics — the main trigger, as they reduce protective gut bacteria
  • Older age, especially over 65
  • A stay in hospital or a care home
  • A weakened immune system or serious underlying illness
  • Certain stomach-acid-reducing medicines

Diagnosis

C. diff is diagnosed with a stool sample that is tested for the bacteria and their toxins. In more serious cases, a scan or a look inside the bowel may be needed to assess how much inflammation is present.

Treatment & management

  • Stopping the antibiotic that triggered the infection, where possible
  • A specific course of antibiotics that target C. diff
  • Staying well hydrated and replacing lost fluids
  • For infections that keep coming back, a faecal microbiota transplant can restore healthy gut bacteria
Recurrence is common: some people get a repeat infection after treatment, so follow-up and careful antibiotic use matter.

Prevention

Good hand hygiene with soap and water (alcohol gels are less effective against C. diff spores), careful and appropriate use of antibiotics, and thorough cleaning in healthcare settings all help reduce the spread.
