Diverticular Disease & Diverticulitis

Small pouches called diverticula can form in the wall of the colon as we get older. They are very common and usually harmless, but can sometimes cause symptoms or become inflamed.

What it is

Diverticular disease and diverticulitis affect the large bowel (colon). Over time, small pouches called diverticula can develop in the bowel wall. Having these pouches is extremely common as we age and is known as diverticulosis.

Most people with diverticula never know they have them. When the pouches cause symptoms such as tummy pain, it is called diverticular disease. If a pouch becomes inflamed or infected, this is diverticulitis.

Key fact: diverticula are a normal part of getting older for many people and usually cause no problems at all. Simple steps, including eating enough fibre, can help ease symptoms and keep them away.

Three terms explained

TermWhat it means
DiverticulosisPouches are present in the bowel, but cause no symptoms
Diverticular diseaseThe pouches cause symptoms such as tummy pain
DiverticulitisOne or more pouches become inflamed or infected

Symptoms

Diverticular disease

  • Tummy pain, usually in the lower left side (a small number of people feel it on the right)
  • Pain that often eases after passing wind or having a poo
  • Bloating
  • Constipation, diarrhoea, or both
  • Occasionally, blood in the stool

Diverticulitis

  • More severe, constant tummy pain (usually on the left)
  • A high temperature (fever)
  • Feeling sick or being sick
  • A change in bowel habit, sometimes with blood or mucus
  • Feeling generally tired and unwell
When to seek help: see your GP if you have ongoing tummy pain or a change in bowel habit. Seek urgent medical advice if you have severe tummy pain with a fever, as diverticulitis sometimes needs prompt treatment.

Causes & risk factors

Diverticula are thought to form when weak spots in the bowel wall give way under pressure, for example from straining with constipation. Factors linked to a higher risk include:

  • A low-fibre diet
  • Getting older, as the bowel wall naturally weakens
  • Constipation and straining
  • Being overweight
  • Smoking
  • Not being very physically active
  • Some painkillers, such as NSAIDs (anti-inflammatories)

Diagnosis

  • Diverticulosis is often found by chance during tests done for another reason
  • A CT scan is the main test if diverticulitis is suspected
  • A colonoscopy or CT colonography may be used to look at the bowel lining
  • Blood tests can check for signs of infection

Treatment & management

Diverticular disease

  • A high-fibre diet and plenty of fluids, increasing fibre gradually
  • Managing constipation
  • Simple pain relief such as paracetamol, avoiding NSAIDs and opioid painkillers, which can upset the bowel

Diverticulitis

  • Mild cases can often be managed at home, sometimes with antibiotics
  • More serious cases may need hospital treatment, and occasionally surgery, to treat or prevent complications

Living with it & prevention

Most people with diverticular disease manage well with simple changes. Eating plenty of fibre, drinking enough fluids, staying active, keeping to a healthy weight and not smoking can all help reduce symptoms and lower the chance of future flare-ups.
