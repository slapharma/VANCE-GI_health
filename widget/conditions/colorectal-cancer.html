Colorectal Cancer

Cancer that begins in the colon or rectum. When found early, often through screening, it is one of the most treatable cancers.

What it is

Colorectal cancer, also called bowel cancer, is cancer that starts in the large bowel (colon) or back passage (rectum). It is one of the most common cancers, yet also one of the most preventable and treatable when caught early.

Most bowel cancers develop slowly from small growths called polyps on the bowel lining. Not all polyps become cancer, but finding and removing them, often during screening, can stop a cancer from ever forming.

Symptoms

  • Bleeding from the back passage, or blood in the stool
  • A persistent change in bowel habit, looser stools or going more often
  • Tummy pain, bloating or discomfort, especially after eating
  • Unintended weight loss
  • Tiredness or breathlessness from unexplained anaemia
Don't wait: these symptoms are usually caused by something far less serious, but if they last more than a few weeks, see your GP. Finding bowel cancer early saves lives.

Risk factors

  • Age: risk rises significantly from the age of 50
  • Family history of bowel cancer or certain inherited conditions
  • A long history of inflammatory bowel disease
  • Lifestyle: diets high in red and processed meat and low in fibre, being overweight, smoking and heavy alcohol use

Screening

Screening looks for early signs of cancer in people who have no symptoms, the single most important way to catch bowel cancer early.

  • FIT (faecal immunochemical test): a simple home test that detects tiny amounts of blood in the stool. In the UK, an NHS bowel screening kit is sent by post to eligible adults within the screening age range.
  • Colonoscopy: offered if a screening test is positive, to look directly at the bowel lining and remove any polyps.
If you receive a screening kit, use it. It takes a few minutes at home and can detect cancer long before any symptoms appear.

Diagnosis

If bowel cancer is suspected, the main test is a colonoscopy, which allows the bowel to be examined and biopsies to be taken. Scans such as CT are then used to check the size of the cancer and whether it has spread (its stage), which guides treatment.

Treatment

Treatment depends on where the cancer is, its stage and a person's general health. The main options, often used in combination, are:

  • Surgery to remove the affected part of the bowel, the most common treatment
  • Chemotherapy
  • Radiotherapy, particularly for rectal cancer
  • Targeted and immunotherapy medicines for some cancers

Prevention

You can lower your risk by eating plenty of fibre, fruit and vegetables, limiting red and processed meat and alcohol, staying a healthy weight, being physically active and not smoking, alongside taking part in screening when invited.
